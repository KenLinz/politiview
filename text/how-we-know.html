How We Know

Politiview gathers public information automatically, and a small AI explains some of it. Nobody here decides what you should think, and we don't always get it right.

Your Privacy

There are no accounts, ads, cookies or trackers here, and we don't collect anything about you. Your browser fetches files from GitHub, and on the globe also from NASA, EOX, Wikimedia and news sites.

Where It Comes From

  • Who runs each country and its parties: Wikidata, checked against Wikipedia's list of current leaders.
  • Election dates and results: Wikipedia's election articles. In the US, also official counts from California, Minnesota and North Carolina.
  • How democratic a country is: V-Dem, Freedom House and the Bertelsmann index, through Our World in Data.
  • News: more than 30 news outlets' public feeds, and Wikipedia's Current Events.
  • Maps: Natural Earth and the US Census.

How The AI Is Limited

  • It only rewrites what news outlets said, in plain words.
  • It may not say who's right, who benefits or what the news proves.
  • Every name and number must appear in its sources, and every sentence must pass a second check against them.
  • Anything that fails isn't shown.
  • It never writes race results.

What We Haven't Done Yet

  • No independent person or group has audited this site.
  • No party has confirmed what we say about it.
  • We haven't tested the site with real people yet.
  • A person hasn't fully checked the country explanations, which were written by AI and compared with Wikipedia by AI.

Mistakes We've Made

6 November 2026. Search called territories like South Georgia and the US Virgin Islands countries, with leaders that could be years out of date, and Paraguay's seats added up to 81 instead of 80. Places we can't double-check are labeled, and seats from a second chamber are no longer counted.

5 November 2026. The plain text pages, and the backup copy of the data built into the site, kept old leaders for hours after the live data was corrected. So did anyone whose browser couldn't reach the live data. Both now update as soon as the data does.

5 November 2026. Leaders' parties came from Wikidata alone, which keeps old parties as if they were current. Examples: Alassane Ouattara, Maia Sandu and Laʻauli Leuatea Schmidt. Each listed leader's party is now compared with their own Wikipedia page on every data update.

5 November 2026. Germany's page called its seats the Bundesrat's, and several countries' seat counts were labeled with the whole parliament's name. Big countries now name the chamber the seats belong to.

5 November 2026. A US House page could say 'Status: Incumbent re-elected' just under a note that nothing had confirmed it, and races with every precinct in said 'Still Counting'. The note is now labeled as Wikipedia's, and those races say they're too close to call or waiting for the final count.

5 November 2026. We logged the claim that unrated places have unfree elections as fixed, but Tuvalu's Parties and Lawmakers pages still said it. It's gone from those pages too. This log was wrong to call it fixed.

5 November 2026. Paraguay, Colombia, Gabon, Belize and Palau counted some parties twice, once for each chamber, which gave seat totals that didn't add up. Each party now counts once, from the chamber listed first.

5 November 2026. Some AI summaries were about something other than the headline above them, and one outlet could be counted twice as sources. A summary now has to be about the headline's subject, or the outlet's own words are shown. Outlets are counted once, and Wikipedia isn't counted as one.

5 November 2026. Winners only Wikipedia named became news headlines. They stay on the map labeled Reported, and aren't news until a state count or two outlets agree.

5 November 2026. Leaders were out of date or wrong in dozens of countries, because they came from one source. Examples: Ghana, Guyana, Zambia, Malawi, Paraguay and Tuvalu. Iran's Supreme Leader was labeled its President, and Palestine showed no leader at all. Every top job is now compared with a second list each time the data updates, and the second list wins when they differ.

5 November 2026. The Dictionary listed China and Cuba as real-world examples of democracy, because their governments use that word. Examples of a kind of government now have to be rated as that kind.

5 November 2026. Places with no research rating, like Tuvalu, were described as having elections that aren't free or fair. Those places now say they aren't rated, which says nothing against them. Palestine's label came from a 1948 rating and was removed.

5 November 2026. AI-written headlines sometimes didn't match their stories, and some summaries said almost nothing. News cards now always use the outlet's own headline. Vague summaries aren't shown, and AI summaries are labeled on the card.

5 November 2026. Winners got a check mark on Wikipedia's word alone, and the top story said Democrats 'now hold' majorities that don't start until January. A check mark now needs a state count or two news outlets, and the wording says when the new Congress starts.

5 November 2026. A 'Confirmed By 25 News Outlets' label counted stories that didn't confirm the claim, like a markets blog, and a 'Wikipedia' link went to another site. Only headlines saying a party won a chamber now count, and links go where they say they go.

5 November 2026. The party page said it showed 'what each one says', but the Republican summary called the party 'far-right' and said some people consider its views prejudiced, while the Democratic summary described the party's goals. A tester pointed this out. That wasn't what the party says, and the two weren't treated alike. Both US parties are now described in the same format from their own 2024 platforms. For every party in every country, plain-language notes can no longer use labels that come from critics.

5 November 2026. Mozambique's prime minister, Maria Benvinda Levy, was shown as the person running the government. President Daniel Chapo is head of government there. We checked other countries with a similar setup and corrected them too.

5 November 2026. Togo's leader was listed under the Rally of the Togolese People. That party was dissolved in 2012. Faure Gnassingbé's party is the Union for the Republic.

5 November 2026. France's president was described as the one who 'runs the government'. France's president and prime minister share power. Both are now described that way.

5 November 2026. Results in countries without free elections, like Laos, read like any other contest. Those pages now say that the seats don't show how much people back each party.

5 November 2026. Indonesia's page said the 1998 financial crisis brought Suharto down. The crisis began in 1997, and huge protests also forced him out in 1998.

5 November 2026. Moldova's 'last election' was actually a vote in Transnistria, a breakaway region. Ireland's 2024 election was missing, and so was Palau's. Votes in breakaway regions no longer count as a country's own, and the missing elections are back.

5 November 2026. Parties were often shown only by initials, like NFP in France, and Washington, DC's mayor was listed as its governor. Parties now use their full names, and DC has a mayor.

4 November 2026. Minnesota's US Senate seat and four of its House seats (MN-02, MN-03, MN-04 and MN-05) were shown as flipping to the Democrats. Democrats already held all five, so none of them flipped. Minnesota's Democrats are called the DFL, and our code didn't read that name in Wikipedia's tables as Democratic.

4 November 2026. The US Virgin Islands and Northern Mariana Islands races for governor were merged into Guam's, so Guam's page listed the Virgin Islands' governor as its own. They are three separate races, and each now has its own record.

4 November 2026. Senators who were appointed, including Jon Husted and Ashley Moody, were missing from their races, so our backup count added up to 104 senators instead of 100. Their records now show who held each seat. The main count on the site came from a different table and wasn't affected.

4 November 2026. An AI summary called Adam Hamawy, an Egyptian-American candidate, a person of Palestinian heritage, and filed the story under Palestine. The sources described him as Egyptian-American. The model guessed the rest from a mention of him volunteering in Gaza. Stories are now placed from their headlines only, and the model is told never to guess anyone's background.

4 November 2026. An AI summary headlined 'Iran Praises U.S. Election Results' blended Israeli and Ukrainian news into the same story, then added a conclusion of its own about how countries choose their governments. The source was a news roundup covering several separate stories. Roundups are no longer summarized at all, and every sentence must now pass a second check against its sources.

4 November 2026. An AI summary said Gadi Eisenkot was the main person trying to form Israel's new government, while another story said the President was still deciding who goes first. The sources did not support the first claim. That story's summary was removed.

4 November 2026. News cards showed who 'gains' and who 'loses' from a story, such as 'Labour Party' gaining from talks on slavery. Those were the AI's own opinions. That feature is gone. The only gain or loss shown now is a seat recorded as changing hands, once it's been confirmed.
